# Strategy: Discover and Define

The first diamond. Discover widens the view, Define narrows it to a few written sentences that steer every visual decision afterwards. Don't design anything in this half; a sketch made now anchors the conversation before the brand knows what it is.

## Discover (read only)

**Purpose:** understand before proposing.

**Inputs:** the inventory, the user's answers, the category the brand competes in.

**Do:**
- **Materials audit** (Path B and C): what the brand already says and shows, and where it contradicts itself. A short inconsistency map is more useful than a long description.
- **Audience:** who chooses, who uses, what they need to believe, where they first meet the brand.
- **Category and benchmarks:** 5–8 brands, mostly direct competitors plus 2–3 aspirational ones from outside the category. Measure, don't just admire: dominant colours (hex), logo type, typefaces and type styles, tone of voice, layout density, imagery style. Then map the category conventions: what everyone does (category codes, which you either follow deliberately or break deliberately) and where the open space is.
- **Past work and reputation** (personal brands): what the person is known for, existing assets, the work that should lead.
- Use web research tools if they are available; otherwise ask the user for links or screenshots of the benchmarks.

**Output** (short):
- 3–5 observations, each one sentence with its evidence;
- where the open space is in the category;
- 3–5 questions whose answers Define needs.

**Gate:** the user accepts the observations and answers the questions.

## Define

Write each item into the log. Keep them short, concrete and testable: a sentence that could describe any brand in the category is not finished.

1. **Name**, if needed (see Naming below).
2. **Positioning statement:** *For [audience] who [need], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [key benefit], because [reason to believe].*
3. **Attributes and what the brand is not:** 3–5 attributes, each paired with what it must not tip into ("precise, not cold"; "warm, not cute"). The "not" side gives the attributes edges and makes them usable as a test.
4. **Core tension:** two opposite qualities the brand holds together, such as "exact form, human voice" or "premium, but never distant". This is the design compass. Typically one side goes to the visual system and the other to the voice or to one expressive element.
5. **Voice:**
   - tone attributes, linked to the attributes above;
   - 3–5 example sentences in different situations: a headline, a product or service description, an apology or error message, a social post;
   - do / don't pairs.
   Short declarative sentences are a safe default for clarity; the brand may want warmth, humour or more rhythm.
6. **Messaging basics:** a one-line description, a boilerplate of about 50 words, an optional tagline. Plus the identity texts collected at intake.
7. **Architecture**, if there is more than one brand or product: monolithic (one brand, descriptors), endorsed (sub-brands with a visible parent), or house of brands (independent). Name the shared DNA.
8. **Visual principles**, as directions rather than values: colour strategy, type approach, mark approach (wordmark, symbol, combination), layout character. These steer Develop.

**Gate:** the user approves the Define sheet in writing. From here on, every visual decision is argued from it: "this fits *precise, not cold* because…".

## Naming (only when needed)

1. **Criteria** from Define: length, sound, languages it must work in, meaning, domain and social handle needs.
2. **Generate widely:** descriptive, invented, metaphor, compound, founder's name, foreign word.
3. **Shortlist** 8–12 names.
4. **Screen** each one: pronunciation in the target languages, unwanted meanings or slang, domain and handle availability, a trademark search in the relevant classes and jurisdictions (for example EUIPO, USPTO, the national office).
5. **Present** 3 finalists, each with its rationale and the risks found.

Say clearly that Claude's screening is preliminary and not legal clearance. Recommend a trademark attorney before the user commits money to the name.

## Personal brands

- The person is the brand: real work and reputation over an invented story. Don't inflate titles or claims.
- Keep headline and actual job title distinct: a headline may be aspirational ("designing calm software"); the title must be true.
- Identity texts (name forms, title, links) in one place, identical on every surface.
- A photo usually works better than a mark as an avatar; the mark is the fallback and the small-size signature.
- Ask early for the person's own sketches or compositions for social surfaces. They often already know what they want to see.
