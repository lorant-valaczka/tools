# Brandbook Builder

A structured brand design process for any brand: company, product, organisation, local business or person. The starting point varies; the destination does not. It ends in a **brandbook** that someone else can pick up and apply without asking the designer, backed by a **decision log** and a **single source of values** (design tokens).

The process is a Double Diamond with gates. First research and define the brand (diverge, converge), then explore and build the identity (diverge, converge). Each phase ends with something the user approves before the next one starts. Gates matter because brand decisions compound: a colour chosen before the positioning is settled, or a logo polished before a direction is picked, usually gets rebuilt.

## Language

Talk to the user in their language. Ask which language the brandbook itself should be written in. It may differ from the conversation (for example, chatting in Hungarian, brandbook in English for an international team).

## Who you're talking to: three levels

People come to brand work with very different vocabularies. "I'll export the logo as SVG" is clear to a designer and meaningless to a bakery owner. Set a level for the person in the conversation, and separately for each group of brandbook readers (an owner at level 1 may hand the book to a printer at level 3):

- **1 · Newcomer:** no design background. Everyday words; a term only when they'll meet it again, explained once. Decisions framed as outcomes. Ready-to-forward messages for suppliers.
- **2 · Familiar:** knows some terms. Common terms used freely, specialist ones explained once.
- **3 · Professional:** the process as described in this skill, in trade terms.

Infer the level from the first message, the person's role and their materials; don't quiz them. State it in half a sentence in the brief message, so they can correct it. If unsure between two levels, pick the lower one, and adjust per topic: if someone uses a term correctly on their own, don't explain it to them.

At level 1, Claude makes the technical decisions (how something is achieved: colour space, file formats, token format, grid unit) and logs them as defaults. **The consequences are never hidden:** cost, time, tasks for the person, limits later on, rights questions. Meaning, taste, money, time and anything customers see stay the person's decisions at every level. The skill's own terms (Path B, gate, Define sheet) are internal; at levels 1 and 2, describe what happens instead.

Rules per level, examples of the same thing said at each level, and a glossary: `references/communication-levels.md`. Read it at intake.

## Step 0: ask about existing materials first

Before proposing anything, find out what already exists. This decides the whole route: the same request ("we need a brand") can mean months of work for one person and a two-day documentation job for another.

Open with a short message: one sentence of heads-up, then two questions, nothing else.

The heads-up, in the user's language: *"A heads-up first: a full brand process is long. It usually runs over several working sessions and uses a fair share of your usage. Once I know what you have, I'll tell you what to expect."* People should know before they invest; the plan at the end of intake then gives the concrete estimate.

1. **Do you already have any brand materials?** For example: a logo or sketches, colours, fonts, an older style guide, a website, social profiles, presentations, photos, a strategy or positioning document, a list of competitors. Share files, links or screenshots, or say that you are starting from zero.
2. **In a sentence or two: who or what is the brand for, and where does it need to work first?**

Keep this message free of design jargon: the user's level isn't known yet. Skip a question only if the user has already answered it. Then follow `references/intake.md`: inventory what came in, choose the path, ask the remaining brief questions in one message, and fill everything else with defaults that are marked as defaults.

## Three paths

| Path | When | How the process changes |
|---|---|---|
| **A · From scratch** | Nothing usable exists, or the user wants a clean start | Full process: strategy, (name), mark, system, applications, brandbook |
| **B · Complete what exists** | Some pieces exist (a logo and a colour but no system; a strategy but no visuals) | Existing pieces become constraints, each marked Keep / Evolve / Replace. Discover includes an audit of what exists; the full process runs for the gaps |
| **C · Document, refresh or apply** | A working brand exists but has no brandbook, the book is outdated, or the brand needs applying to a product or design system | Audit first. Define is extracted from existing material and confirmed, not invented. Develop is skipped (document) or becomes evolution levels (refresh) |

State the path and the phase plan in one short message and get a yes before starting. If the materials don't fit one path neatly, say which parts follow which path (for example, B for the visuals, A for the strategy).

## Phases and gates

| # | Phase | What happens | Output | Gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Intake | materials, inventory, path, brief | brief and inventory in the decision log | user confirms path and plan |
| 2 | Discover (read only) | study the materials, audience and category; 5–8 benchmarks, measured (colours, proportions, type), not just admired | 3–5 observations with evidence; where the open space is; 3–5 questions | observations accepted, questions answered |
| 3 | Define | positioning, attributes and what the brand is not, core tension, voice with example sentences, architecture, (name), visual principles | Define sheet in the decision log | user approves it in writing |
| 4 | Develop | 2–3 directions as quick sketches (style tiles), all on the same fixed test set | directions side by side | one direction chosen, plus what carries over from the others |
| 5 | Build out | chapter by chapter: mark, typography, colour, grid, imagery, (patterns, motion); each with rule, specimen, values, do/don't; usually 2–4 rounds per chapter | finished chapters, tokens | each chapter closed with the checklist |
| 6 | Apply | real surfaces in a deliberate order | applications | review per surface; rule changes written back to the book |
| 7 | Audit | the whole system read end to end | numbered list of errors, stale values, contradictions | user answers each point ("1B, 2A…") |
| 8 | Brandbook and handoff | assemble, export, document | brandbook, tokens, asset files, templates, decision log | closing checklist passes |

On Path C, phase 4 is skipped when documenting; on a refresh it becomes 2–3 change levels (light touch, evolution, bold) shown on the same test set next to the current brand.

Scale the depth to the brand. A sole trader or a personal brand rarely needs more than the minimum brandbook, one round of directions and three applications. A multi-product company needs brand architecture, contextual colour and a token pipeline. Agree the scope at intake and don't silently grow it.

## Working rules

Each of these comes from a real failure in brand projects and prevents it from happening again.

**One decision per question, a few per message.** A decision point is one question with 2–3 numbered options, the recommended one first with a one-line reason. Never fold two decisions into one question: bundled decisions get bundled answers, and the details get lost. A numbered list of separate decision points is fine (the user answers "1A, 2B"), but keep it to about five per message. If there are more, send the ones that block the next step first and say how many will follow.

**Short messages, the user's part first.** The person on the other side is often not a designer and may be reading on a phone. Open with what they need to answer or approve, then only the context they need to answer it. Aim for roughly one screen, about 300 words plus the decision list. Analysis, value tables and full drafts (a Define sheet, a chapter) go into the log or a separate page; the message summarises them in a few lines and points to them. If a message has to carry decisions, questions and requests together, put them under separate headings in that order. Don't send a long draft for review and a new set of decisions in the same message.

**Sketch before you build.** Nothing goes into production tools (Figma, final files, a component library) until a direction is chosen. Directions are cheapest as HTML sketches or rough boards. Building directions properly and then rebuilding them is the most common waste in this kind of work.

**The decision log is the truth.** Keep `<brand>-brand-log.md` from the first exchange: brief, inventory, foundations, decisions (date, decision, rejected alternatives, why), parked items, open questions, session log. The brandbook, tokens and design files implement the log. If they disagree, the log wins and the others get fixed. A decision is written down, dated, in the same session it's made; nothing lives only in the chat. Templates and rules: `references/decision-log.md`.

**One source of values.** Every colour, size, spacing and font value comes from one tokens file, and everything else reads from it. After any decision changes, search every file for the old value (colour code, angle, size, title, status). Stale values are the most common defect in finished brandbooks.

**Proposals stay proposals until approved.** When you suggest a system or a meaning for an element (what a pattern stands for, a scale, a metric), label it as a proposal. The user often accepts because it sounds relevant, while their own picture of the element quietly moves somewhere else. So:
- When starting an element, write two one-sentence purposes side by side, "what is this element for?", the user's and yours. Only build a system on it if the two match.
- Feedback on the form is not approval of the meaning layer underneath it, and silence is not approval. Every layer you add (meaning, scale, metric) needs its own yes.
- When closing a part, ask: "do we mean the same thing by this element?"
- Warning signs of drift: "good idea, but it slows things down" or "too abstract"; several rounds of form-only feedback; you building tools on an unconfirmed premise.
- If two understandings exist, write both down and merge them with one decision, then search for traces of the dropped one. A pattern that often works: the stricter reading softens into a default that the user, or a legend, can override.

**Detours are part of the process; parking is a decision.** Brand design is exploration. Material that has no place in the current version goes to the parking list in the log, not the bin. Record its essence in one sentence (kept separate from where it might go), why it is parked (scale, asset quality, missing system, conflict with a rule), what would unblock it, and what exists (rounds, assets, open decisions). Don't predict where it will land; parked material often returns in a different role than intended. Re-read the list at every new chapter or surface kickoff, when planning a version, and whenever an element fails twice in a row on the same problem. It is ripe when a real problem pulls it in rather than you pushing it, its essence still holds, the original blocker is gone, its new role fits the rules without exceptions, or the user brings it back unprompted.

**Applications write rules back.** Applying the brand to a real surface (website, packaging, deck) almost always produces new rules or exceptions. When a surface closes, ask what changed in the rules, update the chapter, the log and the tokens, and bump the brandbook version. A book that says 1.0 while its tokens say 1.2 is the sign this was skipped.

**Defaults are offers.** The defaults in the reference files suit a restrained, systematic brand. The brand's personality may overrule them: a children's brand, a festival or a bakery will need different answers. Say when you are using a default and why it fits this brand.

**No invented facts.** Don't make up names, claims, numbers, client lists or credentials. Use a marked placeholder (`[TBD: founding year]`) and add it to the open questions.

**Exceptions are named.** Every exception to a rule has a name, lives in one place and is documented in the book. Example: "image-like figures (diagrams, UI illustrations) are treated as images, so the colour rule doesn't apply to them."

## Tools: Figma is optional

At intake, ask which tools the user, and the people who will use the brandbook, work with, and check which tools are available in this environment. Then pick the lightest setup that gets the brandbook into their hands:

- **No design tool, or no preference:** sketches and the brandbook as HTML (an artifact where the environment supports it, otherwise a local `.html` file), logos as SVG, tokens as JSON plus CSS variables, a PDF export of the book if needed. This is enough for a complete brandbook.
- **Figma**, if the user wants it and it is connected: only after a direction is chosen, never for the directions themselves. Build a separate brand variable collection, text styles, and mark, wordmark and lockup components. The book can live in Figma, or stay in HTML with Figma as the asset library. Keep Figma values in sync with the tokens file.
- **Other tools** (Canva, Adobe, Affinity, Sketch, Google Slides or Docs, PowerPoint or Word): deliver specs, asset exports (SVG, PNG, PDF) and templates in formats the tool can import, and put every value in the book so it can be entered by hand.
- **Image generators:** good for moodboards and photography direction. A generated image is never the final logo; the mark must be clean vector artwork, owned, and checked for originality.

Be honest about drawing. Claude can draw geometric, typographic and simple symbolic marks as SVG and refine them precisely. For illustrative, hand-drawn or highly detailed marks, Claude develops the concepts, rough sketches and a drawing brief; the final drawing is better done by the user or an illustrator, and Claude then builds the system around it.

## The output

The final deliverable is a **brandbook**: the rules, specimens and values someone needs to apply the brand correctly without the designer in the room.

Minimum contents (anything less is not a brandbook): idea and voice · mark usage · typography with roles · colour with roles and contrast · layout grid and spacing · at least three real applications · tokens from one source. Imagery, patterns, motion and secondary colours are optional, except where a key surface depends on them: motion and interaction states for a digital-first brand, photography for a hotel or restaurant. Structure, formats and the chapter pattern are in `references/brandbook.md`.

Delivered alongside the book: token files, logo files (every variant, SVG and PNG, the small-size cut, favicon and avatar), templates for the agreed surfaces, font and image licence notes, and the decision log. When any reader is at level 1 or 2, also: a who-gets-which-file page, ready-to-forward messages for the suppliers involved, and a glossary.

## Sessions and parallel work

A brand process doesn't fit in one conversation: it runs over many sessions, and the context of a long conversation eventually fills up and gets compressed. Files are what survive, so the process is built to resume from files, not from chat history. Full rules and templates: `references/decision-log.md`.

- **The hub and the workstreams.** The brand log is the hub: brief, foundations, the list of workstreams, cross-stream decisions, a session log. When work splits (typography in one session, iconography in another), each workstream gets its own file in `log/`. A small single-thread project needs only the hub.
- **One writer per file.** A session writes only its own workstream file, and touches the hub only at its start and end, after reading it again. Each stream owns its token groups. Anything that changes another stream's area goes to the hub as a cross-stream proposal and waits for the user's yes.
- **When to switch sessions:** at the end of a phase or chapter, before starting a parallel stream, and when the conversation gets long. Hand off while everything is still in view.
- **Closing a session:** update the workstream file (status, dated decisions, open items, "resume here"), add a row to the hub's session log, regenerate the tokens, save every produced file, and give the user a ready-to-paste starter message for the next session.
- **Opening a session:** read the hub, the workstream file and the tokens, not the old conversation; apply cross-stream decisions made since; say in a sentence where you're resuming.
